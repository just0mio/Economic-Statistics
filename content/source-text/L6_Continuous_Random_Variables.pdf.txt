Continuous Random Variables




   Continuous random variable: A random variable whose possible values fill an interval, so
   they cannot be listed.
   Examples: a price, a wage, the amount of rainfall, the time it takes to drive to school, the
   labor force participation rate (any number between 0% and 100%).
   Each single value has probability zero, so the math uses calculus instead of sums.

Uniform: An Example


A delivery is equally likely to arrive at any time between 9:00 and 11:00. Let X be the arrival
time in minutes after 9:00, so X ∼ U(0, 120).
    Arrives before 9:30: P(X < 30) = 30/120 = 0.25.
    Arrives exactly at 10:00: P(X = 60) = 0. Only intervals have positive probability.
                                                                        √
    Expected arrival: E (X ) = 60, i.e., 10:00. Standard deviation: 120/ 12 ≈ 34.6 minutes.
    90th percentile: 0.9 × 120 = 108, i.e., 10:48. You can be 90% sure the delivery arrives by
    then.
    The standard uniform is the building block of simulation: RAND() in Excel and runif()
    in R draw from U(0, 1).

Linear Functions of a Normal


   Any random variable has E (a + bX ) = a + b E (X ) and Var(a + bX ) = b 2 Var(X ).
   Normal random variables have an extra property: a linear function of a normal is also
   normal,
                      X ∼ N(µ, σ 2 ) ⇒ a + bX ∼ N(a + bµ, b 2 σ 2 ).
   Example: X ∼ N(2, 9). Then

                     X + 1 ∼ N(3, 9),          2X ∼ N(4, 36),    −2X ∼ N(−4, 36).

   Nonlinear functions of a normal are generally not normal. For example, X 2 cannot be
   negative.

Finding Standard Normal Probabilities

    A standard normal table gives Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z). Example: P(Z ≤ 1.00) = 0.8413 (row
    1.0, column 0.00).
    Useful rules:
         Complement: P(Z > z) = 1 − Φ(z).
         Symmetry: P(Z > z) = P(Z < −z), so Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z).
         Intervals: P(a ≤ Z ≤ b) = Φ(b) − Φ(a).
    P(−1.5 ≤ Z ≤ 2.0) = 0.9772 − 0.0668 = 0.9104.
    P(Z ≥ 1.75) = 1 − 0.9599 = 0.0401, or equivalently P(Z ≤ −1.75) = 0.0401.
    Excel: NORM.S.DIST(z,TRUE) and NORM.DIST(x,µ,σ,TRUE). R: pnorm(z) and
    pnorm(x,mu,sigma).
    Sketch the area first. Most errors are set-up errors, not table errors.

Percentiles: Working Backwards


   Sometimes we know the probability and want the value. The q quantile of X ∼ N(µ, σ 2 )
   is
                                      xq = µ + zq σ,

Independence

   X and Y are independent if the joint PMF equals the product of the marginals for every
   pair of values:
                                   p(x, y ) = pX (x) pY (y ).
   Equivalently, the conditional distribution of Y given X does not depend on X .
   Roulette, same spin: P(yRed = 1, y14 = 35) = 1/37, but
   P(yRed = 1) P(y14 = 35) = (18/37)(1/37). Not independent: knowing that red won
   changes the chance that 14 won.
   Roulette, different spins: the results of separate games are independent.
   The marginal distributions alone do not determine the joint distribution. Two players
   betting on red and black have the same marginals, but one always loses when the other
   wins.

Covariance

   Covariance: A measure of how X and Y move together,

                        Cov(X , Y ) = E [(X − µX )(Y − µY )] = E (XY ) − µX µY .

   Positive: above-average X tends to come with above-average Y . Negative: they move in
   opposite directions. Zero: no linear co-movement.

Correlation


    The size of the covariance depends on the units of X and Y . Correlation removes the
    units:
                                           Cov(X , Y )
                      ρXY = Corr(X , Y ) =             ,   −1 ≤ ρXY ≤ 1.
                                             σX σY
    ρ = 1 or −1: an exact linear relationship. ρ = 0: uncorrelated.
    Roulette: σRed ≈ 1.0 and σ14 ≈ 5.84 (Lecture 5), so ρ ≈ 0.999/(1.0 × 5.84) ≈ 0.17. A
    positive but weak relationship.
    If X and Y are independent, then Cov(X , Y ) = 0.
    The converse fails: if X is symmetric around 0 and Y = X 2 , then Cov(X , Y ) = 0, but Y
    is completely determined by X . Covariance only measures linear association.

Diversification: A Two-Asset Portfolio

Stock: mean 8%, sd 20%. Bond: mean 3%, sd 5%. Correlation 0.2. Portfolio: 60% stock,
40% bond.
    Mean: 0.6(8) + 0.4(3) = 6%.
    Covariance: 0.2 × 20 × 5 = 20.
    Variance: 0.62 (400) + 0.42 (25) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(20) = 144 + 4 + 9.6 = 157.6, so sd ≈ 12.6%.
    This is less than the weighted average of the sds, 0.6(20) + 0.4(5) = 14%.
    Diversification: whenever ρ < 1, the portfolio’s risk is below the weighted average of the
    individual risks.
    With n equally weighted assets, the variance falls toward ρσ 2 , not to zero. Risk shared by
    all assets (a recession) cannot be diversified away. A sample mean is a portfolio of
    observations.

Summary


   A continuous random variable has P(X = x) = 0. Probabilities are areas under the PDF,
   F (x) = P(X ≤ x), and quantiles invert the CDF.
   Uniform: equally likely values on [a, b]. Exponential: waiting times between Poisson
   events.
   Normal: bell-shaped, E (X ) = µ, Var(X ) = σ 2 . Standardize with Z = (X − µ)/σ and use
   Φ. Work backwards with xq = µ + zq σ. Remember 1.645 / 1.96 / 2.576.
   The joint distribution gives marginals and conditionals. Independence means the joint
   PMF is the product of the marginals.
   Covariance and correlation measure linear co-movement.
   Var(aX + bY ) = a2 σX2 + b 2 σY2 + 2ab Cov(X , Y ), which explains diversification.
