Theory Needs Data, Data Need Theory



   Economic theory alone: tells us a demand curve slopes down, but not how steeply
   Data alone: patterns without interpretation — correlations that may mislead
   The scientific loop in economics:

              theory ↑ testable hypothesis ↑ data & statistics ↑ revise theory

   Example: Malthus predicted food per capita would fall; two centuries of yield data
   rejected the prediction — and the reasons (technology) reshaped growth theory

Two Views of What Probability Means


    Frequentist: a probability is a long-run relative frequency
         “P(heads) = 0.5” means: flip the coin forever and half the flips come up heads
         The unknown parameter (µ, p, ω) is a fixed constant; the data are random
         Question asked: what would happen if I repeated this study many times?
    Bayesian: a probability is a degree of belief, updated by evidence
         “P(rain tomorrow) = 0.3” — tomorrow happens once; there is no long run
         The data are what they are; our uncertainty about the parameter is itself described by a
         probability distribution
         Question asked: what should I believe, given what I have seen?
Both camps use the same rules of probability. They disagree about what the numbers are
attached to.

Where the Two Camps Came From
   1763. Thomas Bayes’ essay is published two years after his death, by Richard Price. Laplace
   independently develops the same idea and spends 50 years applying it — to astronomy, to birth ratios,
   to court judgments.
   19th century. This was probability: it was called “inverse probability” and nobody thought it
   controversial. The objection was that the prior often had to be invented.
   1920s–30s. R. A. Fisher (likelihood, p-values, experimental design) and Neyman & Pearson
   (hypothesis tests, confidence intervals) build the frequentist framework explicitly to avoid priors. It wins
   the textbooks and the journals — partly on principle, largely because it could be computed with a table
   and a pencil.
   1930s–50s. Bayesian revival on philosophical grounds: de Finetti, Je!reys (who argued the point
   with Fisher in print for years), and Savage show that coherent beliefs must obey the probability rules.
   1990s–today. Cheap computing and MCMC make Bayesian analysis practical; it is now standard in
   forecasting, macro (Bayesian VARs), and machine learning. Economics remains mostly frequentist —
   the tradition this course sits in.

Monty Hall: Switch

Your first pick was Door 1. Three equally likely states of the world:

              Car is behind    Probability                Host must open        Sticking wins?
                   Door 1             1/3            Door 2 or 3 (his choice)        ↭
                   Door 2             1/3               Door 3 (forced)              —
                   Door 3             1/3               Door 2 (forced)              —

    Sticking wins 1/3 of the time. Switching wins 2/3. Switching doubles your chances.
    Your original door was right with probability 1/3, and nothing the host does can change
    that — he was always going to be able to show you a goat. So the remaining 2/3
    collapses onto the one unopened door.
    The host’s rule is what makes this work. If he opened a door at random and it happened
    to show a goat, it really would be 50–50.

Why Monty Hall Is on This Slide

   Intuition about conditional probability is unreliable. When Marilyn vos Savant
   published the correct answer in 1990, she received thousands of letters telling her she was
   wrong — many from people with PhDs. Paul Erdős, one of the great mathematicians of
   the century, reportedly stayed unconvinced until he was shown a simulation.
   Evidence has to be conditioned on properly. Formally this is Bayes’ rule:
   P(car behind 2 | host opens 3) = 2/3. We will do this carefully when we take up
   conditional probability.
   How the data were generated determines what you can conclude. The numbers on
   the doors never changed — what mattered was the rule the host was following. Change
   the rule and the answer changes.
   That last point is the whole course in miniature: a dataset alone does not tell you what it
   means. You need to know where it came from.

Populations and Samples



   Population: the entire set of units we want to learn about (all households, all firms, all
   transactions)
   Sample: the subset of the population we actually observe
   Parameter: a numerical feature of the population (usually unknown), e.g. µ, ε 2 , p
   Statistic: a numerical feature of the sample, e.g. x̄, s 2 , p̂
   Core problem of statistics: what can we say about the population from a sample?

Census vs. Sample


   Why not always observe the whole population?
       Cost and time (a census of 20 million households is expensive)
       Sometimes impossible (testing every battery destroys every battery)
       Populations can be conceptual: all potential customers, all future harvests
   A well-drawn sample of 1,000 can beat a sloppy census: how you sample matters more
   than how much
   Korea’s Population and Housing Census: conducted every 5 years, with the
   complete-enumeration component now register-based; quinquennial sample surveys
   provide additional detailed information. Most economic surveys are samples.

Descriptive vs. Inferential Statistics



    Descriptive statistics: summarizing and presenting the data at hand
        Tables, graphs, means, standard deviations
    Inferential statistics: drawing conclusions about a population from a sample
        Estimation, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests
    The bridge between them is probability: it tells us how samples behave when drawn from
    a known population — inference reverses the direction

Categorical Variables

Values are labels, not amounts. Arithmetic on them is meaningless.
    Nominal — categories with no natural order
         region, industry (KSIC code), crop planted, occupation, marital status
         only counts and proportions make sense: “38% of firms are in manufacturing”
    Ordinal — categories with a meaningful order, but unknown or unequal spacing
         education level (HS < BA < MA < PhD), credit rating (AAA > AA > A), Likert scales,
         income brackets
         order is real: “at least a bachelor’s degree” is a meaningful statement
    Binary (dichotomous) — a special case with exactly two categories
         employed / not, owns home / not, treated / control
         coded 0/1 it becomes a dummy variable, and then the mean is meaningful: it is the
         proportion

Quantitative Variables: Discrete vs. Continuous

Values are amounts; di”erences and (often) ratios are meaningful.
    Discrete: a countable set of values — usually the answer to “how many?”
          number of children, employees, store visits, accidents per year
          gaps between possible values are real: no household has 2.4 children
          modeled with the discrete distributions we meet later (Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson)
     Continuous: any value in an interval — the answer to “how much?”
          income, price, yield per hectare, time, temperature
          any two values have a value between them; we only record it rounded
          modeled with continuous densities (uniform, normal)

Borderline cases are normal. Money is technically discrete (won are whole units) but we treat it as
continuous. A count with many possible values (population of a city) is treated as continuous too. The
choice is about which model is convenient, not about metaphysics.

Ordinal Data: Why the Mean is Slippery

Course satisfaction from 50 students. Coding A spaces the categories evenly; Coding B

Common Mistakes with Variable Types


   Averaging category codes. Region coded 1–17: the “mean region” of 8.3 means
   nothing. The single most common spreadsheet error.
   Numbers that are not quantities. Postal codes, student IDs, phone numbers, KSIC
   industry codes — numerals used as names.
   Missing-value codes treated as data. Surveys often code “refused” as ↓99 or 9999;
   average them in and your mean income is negative.
   Throwing away information. Binning a continuous variable (exact income ↑ five
   brackets) is sometimes useful, but it is a one-way door: you cannot recover the detail.
   Ignoring units and deflation. “Price” in won 2005 and won 2026 are not the same
   variable.

Anatomy of an Empirical Study


Every empirical paper — and your problem sets — follow the same skeleton:
  1   Question: sharp, answerable with data
  2   Data: source, sample, unit of observation, key variables and their types
  3   Descriptive statistics: means, spreads, plots — know your data first
  4   Method: comparison, test, or regression — with assumptions stated
  5   Results: estimates with standard errors and intervals
  6   Interpretation: magnitude, significance, caveats

Data Ethics and Privacy




   Microdata describe real people and firms: confidentiality is a legal and ethical duty
   Anonymization, aggregation, and secure data rooms are standard practice
   Report honestly: no selective deletion of observations, no undisclosed specification search
   Reproducibility: keep code and data so others can verify your results
   These norms are enforced — in academia, government, and industry alike

Frequency Tables



   A frequency table lists each value (or bin) and how often it occurs
   Relative frequency: frequency divided by the number of observations n

Chebyshev’s Inequality and the Empirical Rule




    Chebyshev (any distribution!): at least 1 ↗ 1/k 2 of observations lie within k sds of the

    Empirical rule (bell-shaped data): about 68% within ±1s, 95% within ±2s, 99.7%
    within ±3s

Example: Empirical Rule


The time for a worker to complete an operation averages 12.8 minutes with a standard
deviation of 1.7 minutes. The distribution is approximately mound-shaped. What proportion
of workers take longer than 16.2 minutes?
    µ = 12.8, ω = 1.7:    16.2 = µ + 2ω = 12.8 + 2(1.7)
    Empirical rule: 95% of observations lie within µ ± 2ω, i.e., between 9.4 and 16.2
    So 5% lie outside this interval; by symmetry, half of that is above 16.2

    About 2.5% of workers take longer than 16.2 minutes

What Do Probabilities Mean?



   Frequentist interpretation: The random process can be repeated many times, and the
   probability of an event is the approximate fraction of times that it occurs. If you bet on
   Red in roulette 1000 times, you will win about 486 times.
   Bayesian interpretation: The random process is a one-time occurrence, and the
   probability represents the strength of our belief that the event will happen.
   The frequentist interpretation suits repeated settings like casino games or car insurance.
   The Bayesian interpretation makes more sense for predicting election results.

Roulette: An Example



   A roulette wheel has slots numbered 0 to 36. Slot 0 is green, 18 slots are red, and 18
   slots are black.
   A ball is spun and lands in one slot. Players place bets such as:
       Red (the ball lands in a red slot): pays $1 per $1 bet.
       Black (the ball lands in a black slot): pays $1 per $1 bet.
       A single number (the ball lands on that number): pays $35 per $1 bet.
   A roulette game is a random process. Something will happen, it matters to the players
   and the casino what will happen, but we do not know in advance what will happen.

   Outcome: A complete description of the result of a random process. Everything we are

Events


   Event (A): A subset of the sample space. Equivalently, a statement about the outcome

Relationships Among Events


   Identical (A = B): The two events contain exactly the same outcomes. “The ball lands
   on 14” and “a bet on 14 wins” are identical.
   Implies (A ↑ B): All outcomes of A are also in B. “The ball lands on 14” implies “the
   ball lands on red” since {14} ↑ Red.
   Disjoint, or mutually exclusive (A ↓ B = ↔): The two events share no outcomes, so they
   cannot both happen. Red ↓ Black = ↔.
   But disjoint events can both fail to happen: if b = 0, neither red nor black wins.
   Any two elementary events are either identical or disjoint.

   possible event.
   All valid probability distributions obey three axioms:
     1   Probabilities are never negative: P(A) ≃ 0.
     2   One of the outcomes will definitely happen: P(!) = 1.
     3   For any two disjoint events A and B: P(A ↗ B) = P(A) + P(B).   AUB   =
                                                                                  A unim B

   All other properties of probability can be derived from these three axioms.

Joint Probability



    We are often interested in how two events are related, e.g., a poker player after seeing the
    cards, or an economist predicting future economic conditions from current data.
    Joint probability: The probability that both A and B happen, P(A ↓ B).
    Roulette: What is the probability that the ball lands on a number that is both red and
    even?
              P(Red ↓ Even) = P({12, 14, 16, 18, 30, 32, 34, 36}) = 8/37 ⇒ 0.216
    Joint probabilities are just probabilities, so they obey all the rules of probability.

Checking Independence


   Events defined on the same outcome are typically related.
   Roulette: P(Red) = 18/37 ⇒ 0.486, but P(Red | Even) = 8/18 ⇒ 0.444. So Red and
   Even are not independent.
   Mutual funds: P(B | A) = 0.275, but P(B) = 0.17. So a top-20 MBA and outperforming
   the market are not independent.
   IT and certification: P(I | C ) = 0.33, but P(I ) = 0.20. Not independent.
   Independence is an assumption, and one that can easily be incorrect. Do not use results
   that only apply under independence when there is no reason to believe it holds.

Quality Control: An Example


   A company has three production lines. Line S1 produces 70% of output with a 4% defect
   rate, S2 produces 20% with a 2% defect rate, and S3 produces 10% with a 96% defect
   rate.
   A randomly selected product is defective (H). What is the probability that it came from
   S1 ?
   Joint probabilities: P(H ↓ S1 ) = 0.028, P(H ↓ S2 ) = 0.004, P(H ↓ S3 ) = 0.096.
   Law of total probability: P(H) = 0.028 + 0.004 + 0.096 = 0.128.
   Bayes’ law: P(S1 | H) = 0.028/0.128 ⇒ 0.219, down from the prior of 0.70.

Summary


   Probability rules: 0 ⇐ P(A) ⇐ 1, P(Ac ) = 1 ↘ P(A), and
   P(A ↗ B) = P(A) + P(B) ↘ P(A ↓ B).
   Conditional probability: P(A | B) = P(A ↓ B)/P(B), and multiplication rule:
   P(A ↓ B) = P(A | B) P(B).
   Independence: P(A ↓ B) = P(A) P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A). Independence is
   not the same as mutually exclusive.

   Random variable (X ): A number whose value depends on a random outcome. Formally, it
   is a function that assigns a number to each outcome in the sample space.
   Economics is a mostly quantitative field: prices, quantities, interest rates, unemployment
   rates, and GDP are all numbers. When a random outcome is described by a number, we
   call it a random variable.
   Notation: A capital letter X denotes the random variable, and a lowercase letter x
   denotes a particular value it can take.
   Examples: The number of heads in 10 coin flips, a farm’s harvest, next month’s sales, or
   an indicator equal to 1 if a loan defaults and 0 otherwise.

When the Binomial Fails


   The binomial requires independent trials with the same p. Check both before using it.
   Sampling without replacement from a small population changes p from draw to draw
   (recall the M&Ms in Lecture 4). The hypergeometric distribution handles this case.
   Loan↓portfolio: 100 loans, each defaulting with p = 0.05. If defaults were independent,
   ω = 100 ↑ 0.05 ↑ 0.95 ↔ 2.2, and 20 or more defaults would be essentially impossible.
   But defaults share common shocks such as recessions, so they are not independent. The
   number of defaults is then far more variable, and 20 or more becomes plausible.
   Assuming independence when it fails understates risk. This was a central lesson of the
   2008 financial crisis.

Summary


   A random variable assigns a number to each random outcome. The PMF
   p(x) = P(X = x) and the CDF F (x) = P(X ≃ x) each describe its distribution
   completely.
                                 %
   Expected value: E (X ) = x x p(x). Linearity: E (a + bX ) = a + b E (X ), but
   E [g (X )] ⇒= g (E (X )) for nonlinear g .
   Variance: Var(X ) = E [(X → µ)2 ] = E (X 2 ) → µ2 , and Var(a + bX ) = b 2 Var(X ).
   Two random variables can have the same mean but very di!erent risk.
   Bernoulli for one binary outcome, binomial for the number of successes in n independent
   trials, Poisson for counts of events at a constant rate, discrete uniform for equally likely
   values.
